Attribution assigns credit. It looks at the touchpoints a customer passed through and divides the sale between them. That is a useful record of what happened. It is not an answer to the question a budget holder is asking.

The budget question is counterfactual: if we removed this channel, how many of these sales would we lose? A channel can appear in most customer journeys and still add little. Branded search is the usual example. People who already intend to buy search for the brand name and click the ad. The ad is present; it is not the cause.

## Presence is not contribution

Last-click, first-click and data-driven models all share one limit. They only see the journeys that exist. They cannot see the sales that would have happened anyway, or the ones that never happened because a channel was switched off.

To measure contribution you need variation you control:

- **Geo experiments.** Turn spend down in comparable regions and compare the outcome.
- **Holdout groups.** Keep a random share of an audience out of a campaign.
- **Marketing mix models.** Use history across many budget changes to estimate each channel's effect, with uncertainty.

## What to do on Monday

Keep attribution for what it is good at: day-to-day optimisation inside a channel. Move budget between channels only on evidence of incremental effect. Start with the largest line in the media plan, run one clean test, and write down the decision rule before the results arrive.
